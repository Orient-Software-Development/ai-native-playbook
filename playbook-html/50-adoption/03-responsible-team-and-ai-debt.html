The Responsible Team and AI Debt

Responsibility doesn't transfer to the model — the human who merges owns the code, full stop. So the team does two things the agent can't: it maps the zones where the agent reliably flops and decides what stays human, and it treats the debt the agent generates as a managed budget — visible, and paid down before it compounds.

Every page so far has been about making the agent more capable. Not one of them makes the agent accountable — and that isn't an oversight, it's the whole point. The agent has no stake in the outcome: no reputation to protect, no 2 a.m. pager, not a flicker of shame at a 300-line function. So the human who hits merge owns the code, full stop. This page is about the two jobs that fall out of that uncomfortable fact: mapping the zones where the agent reliably flops, and managing AI debt before it quietly compounds into a codebase nobody can change.

01The principle

Everything in this playbook makes the agent more capable. None of it makes the agent responsible. The agent has no stake in the outcome — no career on the line, no recoil at a 300-line function. It optimises for the task in front of it and moves on. That means it can never be the party accountable for what ships. The human who merges the change is the author of record — not the model that wrote it.

This isn't a moral nicety; it's load-bearing. The "humans steer, agents execute" division of labour only works if a human is genuinely steering. Drop that, and you get the failure mode where debt compounds invisibly precisely because no one feels accountable for code they didn't write. Two responsibilities follow: allocate trust by zone, not uniformly, and manage AI debt as a budget.

02Why it works

Uniform trust over-trusts the agent exactly where it's weakest. The agent is genuinely strong in well-harnessed, verifiable zones — typed CRUD against a real test suite — and genuinely weak in others. Treat it as uniformly trustworthy and you apply the same light review to a routine list endpoint and a change to how money is calculated. The routine change didn't need you; the money change needed you most, and got the same glance. Mapping the flop zones is how you put human attention where it has the most leverage.

Subjective frontend & design

Weakest where it's most confident — it declares a screen "looks right" without being able to see it, and gravitates to bland layouts. The self-verify blind spot.

Cross-cutting refactors

It replicates the patterns already in the repo — including the bad ones — so a sweeping change spreads existing flaws and drifts the architecture rather than cleaning it up.

Ambiguous business rules

A vague spec doesn't make the agent cautious, it makes it confidently wrong: it fills the gap with a plausible guess and writes tests that confirm the guess.

Security-sensitive code

Auth, permissions, data exposure — the stakes are high and the failure is plausible-looking, the worst combination for a skim review.

Four flop zones that recur across codebases — name them, and put human attention where it pays

AI debt compounds invisibly, so it needs a process, not goodwill. The agent produces plausible code faster than humans can carefully read it, so unreviewed shortcuts pile up. And because no one wrote the code, no one feels the ownership that normally makes an engineer go back and clean it up — the debt has no one to pay it. Left alone it compounds until the codebase is one neither human nor agent can change safely.

Debt is a high-interest loan — pay it continuously. It's almost always cheaper to pay debt down in small, regular increments than to let it accumulate and tackle it in a painful burst. The "we'll spend a day a quarter cleaning up the slop" approach doesn't scale; by the quarter mark the slop has already spread into everything built on top of it.

Capture the standard once, enforce it continuously — the burst version compounds before you reach it

Whatever the agent writes, a person still owns the merge. Responsibility is the one thing the harness can't externalise.

03How to apply it

  • Write the flop zones down, with a harness plan for each. Don't keep "the agent is bad at design" in your head — name the zone and decide its controls. Frontend/design → a design guide and evidence images, graded by a separate evaluator. Refactors → a human owns the scope; lean on fitness functions to catch drift. Business rules → pin the rule in a normative spec first. Security → mandatory human review, never auto-merge.
  • For each zone, decide: automate, guard, or keep human. The default of "trust it everywhere" is a decision too, just an unexamined one.
Automate

The verifiable

Typed CRUD against a real test suite — let the agent run, because a miss is caught cheaply by a strong sensor.

Guard

The regressable

Let the agent work, but stand a sensor — a fitness function, a boundary rule — between it and the trunk to catch drift the diff hides.

Keep human

The subjective & high-stakes

Security and money logic stay human — never auto-merged. The one zone where the gate is a person, on purpose, maybe indefinitely.

Decide each zone deliberately — automate the verifiable, guard the regressable, keep the dangerous human

  • Earn autonomy zone by zone. Tie this to growing the harness: a zone earns more autonomy as its controls prove they catch mistakes. Some zones (security, money) may stay human indefinitely, on purpose.
  • Make AI debt visible — in the repo, not in someone's head. Keep a tracked list of the known shortcuts and weak areas. A debt in the system of record can be scheduled; a debt in a Slack thread is invisible the moment the thread scrolls away.
  • Schedule paydown on a continuous cadence. Pay down a little every cycle. Cleanup is itself good agent work — point the agent at the tracked debt on a regular rhythm and review the small diffs.
  • Keep a named human accountable for every merge. "Who owns this if it breaks?" must always have an answer that is a person.
  • Don't: auto-merge on green across the board as if the agent were uniformly reliable; treat responsibility as something the model now carries; let debt accumulate for a someday cleanup; or hand a security or business-rule change to the agent end-to-end with no human gate.

04In practice

A team has a fast, productive agent workflow and a habit they're proud of: if CI is green, it merges. It's served them well on dozens of routine features. Then the agent picks up a change to billing — how a subscription is prorated when a customer upgrades mid-cycle.

Uniform auto-trust

Merge on green, everywhere

  • The billing spec was a loose paragraph — it never pinned how proration rounds, or what a same-day upgrade does.
  • The agent filled the gap with a guess, and — because it also wrote the tests — those tests assert the guess. CI is green.
  • The change ships with the same glance a list endpoint would get.

Customers are mischarged on upgrade, and the post-mortem's hardest question — who owned this? — has no honest answer, because responsibility was left with the model.

Stay accountable

Two flop zones at once, treated as such

  • Money logic is never auto-merged; its spec must state the rounding and edge cases normatively before any code.
  • The vague paragraph gets sent back and pinned down; a human signs off that it matches what the business wants.
  • The change gets a real review, not a glance — and the wrong proration assumption surfaces there.

The mistake is caught while it's a paragraph and a diff, not a billing run — and the small shortcuts elsewhere are tracked and paid down weekly.

The agent isn't uniformly trustworthy — pretending it is spends your least attention where you needed the most

The lesson the example carries: the defence isn't to trust the agent less everywhere — it's to decide, on purpose and zone by zone, what's automated, what's guarded, and what stays human. And whatever the agent writes, a person still owns the merge. Responsibility is the one thing the harness can't externalise.

05Anti-patterns

  • Uniform auto-trust. Merging on green everywhere, as if a billing change and a list endpoint carried the same risk. The agent's reliability is not uniform, so neither should your trust be.
  • "The model is responsible." Treating accountability as transferred to the agent. A merge with no human owner is a miss with no one to catch it and a debt with no one to pay it.
  • The invisible debt. Shortcuts and known-weak areas that live in people's heads instead of a tracked list. What isn't written down can't be scheduled, and compounds silently.
  • The big cleanup day. Letting debt batch up for a quarterly "slop sprint" instead of paying it down continuously. By the time the sprint arrives, the debt has spread into everything built on top of it.
  • The un-gated flop zone. Handing the agent a security or business-rule change end-to-end with no human in the loop — full autonomy granted to exactly the zone that hasn't earned it.
